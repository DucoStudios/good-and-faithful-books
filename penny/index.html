Penny and the Golden Notebook

Nine-year-old Penny has four weeks to raise $240 for sailing camp, and her great-grandfather left her the instructions, sealed in wax. A chapter book series where kids learn real lessons about money, work and kindness by watching Penny try, fail and figure it out.

Why ages 7 to 10

The age when kids decide what money is for

Between 7 and 10, kids start to notice money: an allowance, what their friends have, price tags at the store. They are also old enough to follow a real chapter book with real stakes. Penny meets them right there. They learn, by watching her try, fail and figure it out, that money is a receipt for helping someone, never the goal.

Penny and the Golden Notebook, chapter 1

Chapter 1. What She Wasn't Supposed to Hear

Penny Lind had decided she wasn't going to fall asleep until she'd finished her book. That was why she was still awake when she heard the voices from the kitchen.

It was almost eleven. In the room next door, Theo was snoring, a small whimpering sound like a sleeping puppy. Penny put down her book and listened.

"...three weeks without a single new job," said Dad. "It's never been like this."

"It'll turn around," said Mom. "It always does."

"And if it doesn't?"

Penny crept out onto the rug. The hallway floor creaked in three places, and she knew exactly where. She stepped over the first one, over the second, and sat down on the top stair with her knees tucked under her nightgown.

Down in the kitchen, paper rustled. Dad was going through the bills. She could tell. He had a special sigh for bills.

"We'll have to cancel camp," he said at last. "For Penny. We don't have two hundred and forty dollars to spare right now."

Penny stopped breathing.

Sailing camp. A whole week next July out on Pine Island, with little sailboats and tents and evening swims and Emma in the bunk next to hers. She had talked about it ever since last summer, when she watched the camp boats sail past the harbor. She had practiced knots on her shoelaces. She could tie a bowline with her eyes closed.

"She's going to be so sad," Mom said quietly.

"I know." Dad's chair scraped against the floor. "We won't tell her yet. The fee is due in four weeks. Something might happen."

Meet the characters

Penny and her friends in Oak Harbor

Penny Lind, 9

Curious, impatient, never gives up. Promises a bit too much, and always sees people.

Emma Hayes, 9

Penny's best friend. Thinks before she speaks, counts every cent, keeps it all working.

Theo, 6

Little brother. Asks "why?" about everything. Takes every job very seriously.

Grandma Rose

Never gives answers, only questions. "And what do you think?"

Mr. Green

Grumpy neighbor with a bad back and the biggest apple tree in Oak Harbor. Soft inside.

Marcus Bell, 10

The rival with the new bike and the soda stand. Not as tough as he looks.

What your child takes away

Problems are opportunities

Penny learns to look for people who need help. That habit is worth more than any lemonade stand.

Money follows service

She gets paid when she solves someone's problem, and not a moment before. Money is a receipt, never the goal.

Faith in everyday choices

Penny makes mistakes and makes them right. Each seal ends with a short Bible verse, never a sermon.

Questions parents ask

What age is Penny and the Golden Notebook for?

Kids 7 to 10 reading on their own, and younger kids as a read-aloud. Short chapters that end with a question or a surprise make it a good bedtime book.

Do we have to read the books in order?

No. Each book stands on its own. Read in order, they tell one big story with a mystery that is solved in book 7.

Is it a Christian book?

Yes. Faith shows up in how Penny and her family think, pray and choose, with a short Bible verse at every seal. The story always comes first and it never preaches.

Isn't money a strange topic for kids?

Kids learn about money anyway, mostly from ads and screens. Penny teaches that money is a receipt for helping someone, and that generosity and faithfulness matter more than getting rich.
